# Script Draft 01

working title

**Best Robot Vacuum 2026? I Compared the Expert Rankings**

## cold open

On September 25th
I checked eight serious
robot-vacuum review sources.

They gave me
eight different answers
to "what is the best robot vacuum?"

RTINGS says Roborock Saros 10R.

Vacuum Wars says MOVA V70 Ultra Complete.

Tom's Guide says Dreame X60 Max Ultra Complete.

WIRED says Shark PowerDetect UV Reveal.

Expert Reviews says Eufy Omni E25.

The Hook Up says Roborock Qrevo Curv 2 Flow.

TechRadar says the older Qrevo Curv.

Good Housekeeping says the S8 MaxV Ultra.

At this point
"best robot vacuum"
has become less of an award
and more of a group project.

So I went through
the testing systems
the comparisons
the long-term owner reports
and the fine print

to figure out

who is wrong

and the annoying answer is

mostly

nobody.

They are quietly answering
different questions.

## intro

This is Review Pirate.

The idea is simple.

Instead of buying one vacuum
using it for three days
and adding review number 4,001
to the internet

we review
the reviews.

For this first one
I pulled together
more than thirty sources

including

large standardized test databases

professional home testing

YouTube comparisons

and long-term owners
who have had enough time
for the robot
to become either

a beloved appliance

or a $1,500
mobile argument.

Links to the sources
are below.

Let's start with
why the experts disagree.

## section one — best among what

The first problem
is almost embarrassingly simple.

You cannot choose
a vacuum
you did not test.

Vacuum Wars
has tested more than 200
robot vacuums
and continually reranks
new models.

And "continually"
is doing work there.

On September 16
its robot-vacuum hub
showed the Dreame X60 Max
at number one.

Eight days later
the Top 20 page
showed the new MOVA V70
at number one.

That is not a scandal.

It is the half-life
of a leaderboard.

A new product
entered the test pool
and the answer changed.

RTINGS
has tested around 100
and currently prefers
the Roborock Saros 10R.

Good Housekeeping's
current best overall
is the older S8 MaxV Ultra.

That does not mean
one lab
forgot how vacuums work.

Their candidate pools
are different.

This matters more
than review sites
usually admit.

"Best"
actually means

best among
the products
this reviewer tested

using this methodology

at this point in time

in this market.

Which is a much uglier headline.

## section two — best at what

Then there is
the scoring problem.

Consider Consumer Reports.

Their current
"best robot vacuum" guide
is specifically
vacuum-only robots.

Robot vacuum-and-mop combos
live in a different category.

They also test
privacy and security

and use member surveys
for reliability
and owner satisfaction.

That is a very different question
from

which $1,500 flagship
has the strongest mopping dock.

The Hook Up
includes things like
local control
and app behavior.

Expert Reviews
runs robots
through the same
58-square-meter home layout

and measures standardized
rice
flour
and pet-hair pickup

while also caring
about price and setup.

WIRED
tests in a real home
with three adults
a preschooler
and a cat.

Which is possibly
the most scientifically rigorous way
to manufacture floor debris.

None of these methods
is obviously wrong.

But once you change
the weights

you change the winner.

## section three — the fake precision problem

This is where
the giant spec numbers
start getting suspicious.

Modern robot vacuums
advertise suction numbers
that look like
they are competing
for a mortgage rate.

18,000 Pa.

35,000 Pa.

42,000 Pa.

And we now have
a wonderfully rude example
of why the number
is not a cross-brand score.

MOVA advertises
one model
at about 19,000 Pa

and another
at about 13,000.

In Vacuum Wars'
own sealed suction test

the 13,000-Pa model
measured higher.

1.08 kPa

versus

0.88.

Another current Roborock
advertised at 36,000 Pa

measured below
Vacuum Wars'
category-average suction

and still cleaned well.

So manufacturer Pa
is measuring something.

It is just not
a universal conversion chart
from bigger number
to cleaner floor.

So one thing
Review Pirate
is going to track
over time

is whether these
marketing metrics
actually predict
anything useful
across brands.

For now

treat Pa
as a clue

not a verdict.

## section four — launch reviews have a time problem

This was
the most interesting part
of the research.

A lot of robot vacuums
look great
for the first week.

Of course they do.

They are clean.

The brushes are new.

The firmware
has not yet discovered
its artistic phase.

One Ecovacs T90 owner
posted a very positive
first-week review.

Then updated it.

In week two
the robot started
getting stuck
losing its way
and leaving areas uncleaned.

They returned it.

That single post
does not prove
the T90 is unreliable.

It proves something
more useful.

A one-week review
cannot answer
a one-year question.

The Saros 10R
has the opposite advantage.

It has been around
long enough
that we now have
three-month
one-year
positive
negative
and extremely annoyed
owner reports.

Some owners love
the low maintenance
navigation
and mopping.

Others complain
about rug fringe
small-object avoidance
dock failures
or weak vacuuming
relative to older models.

That disagreement
is not noise
to delete.

It tells us
which homes
stress the system differently.

## section five — obstacle avoidance is not one thing

This comes up constantly.

A reviewer
can run a beautiful
repeatable obstacle course.

Cables.

Shoes.

Fake pet waste.

Toys.

And that test
is genuinely useful.

But a family home
with a one-year-old
a four-year-old
rug fringe
reflective floors
and toys that migrate
while the robot is running

is a different distribution.

One long-term Saros 10R owner
basically gave up
using it
outside the kitchen

because the rest
of the home
was too chaotic.

Another owner
runs theirs every day
and reports
excellent object avoidance.

Those reports
can both be true.

"Obstacle avoidance"
is not one scalar number.

The hidden variable
is the house.

## section six — so what actually matters

After reading all this

I would not start
with a brand.

I would start
with your failure mode.

If your floors
are mostly hard surfaces
and mopping quality
is the reason
you want a robot

the roller-mop systems
deserve extra attention.

If your house
is full of kids
pets
cords
and floor clutter

obstacle avoidance
matters more
than theoretical suction.

If your house
is carpet-heavy

the best mopping system
on Earth
is not the center
of your problem.

If you hate maintenance

look at the dock

the dirty-water path

the hair handling

and what owners say
after three months.

If privacy
and cloud dependence
matter

Consumer Reports
and reviewers
who test local control
become much more useful
than a cleaning-only leaderboard.

And if value matters

use the current street price

not the launch price.

A $1,300 robot
discounted to $700

is a different product decision
without changing
a single screw.

## section seven — who won

So.

What is
the best robot vacuum
of 2026?

I am not going
to manufacture
a ninth winner
just to finish the video.

That would be
extremely traditional
review-site behavior.

The useful conclusion
is this.

The top reviewers
mostly agree
on what matters.

Cleaning.

Mopping.

Navigation.

Obstacle avoidance.

Maintenance.

Software.

Price.

Where they disagree
is how much
each one matters

and which products
they have tested
recently enough
to compete.

The disagreement
is the buying guide.

If we can map
your house
your floor types
your tolerance
for maintenance
your privacy preferences
and your budget

the list gets
a lot shorter.
