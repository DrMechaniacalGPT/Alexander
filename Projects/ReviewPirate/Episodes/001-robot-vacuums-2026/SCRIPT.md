# Script Draft 02

working title

**Best Robot Vacuum 2026? Why the Experts Don’t Agree**

## cold open

On September 25
I checked eight serious
robot-vacuum review sources.

They gave me
eight different answers
to one extremely normal question.

What is
the best robot vacuum?

RTINGS:
Roborock Saros 10R.

Vacuum Wars:
MOVA V70 Ultra Complete.

Tom's Guide:
Dreame X60 Max Ultra Complete.

WIRED:
Shark PowerDetect UV Reveal.

Expert Reviews:
Eufy Omni E25.

The Hook Up:
Roborock Qrevo Curv 2 Flow.

TechRadar:
the older Qrevo Curv.

Good Housekeeping:
S8 MaxV Ultra.

At this point
"best robot vacuum"
is less of an award
and more of a group project.

So I went through
more than thirty sources
to figure out
who is wrong.

The annoying answer is:

mostly nobody.

They're answering
different questions.

## what does "best" even mean

First problem:

you cannot choose
a vacuum
you did not test.

Vacuum Wars
has tested more than 200 models
and keeps reranking new ones.

On September 16
its robot-vacuum hub
had the Dreame X60 Max
at number one.

Eight days later
its Top 20
had the new MOVA V70
at number one.

That isn't scandal.

That's the half-life
of a leaderboard.

A new candidate arrived.
The answer changed.

RTINGS
has tested around 100
and currently prefers
the Saros 10R.

Good Housekeeping
still prefers
the older S8 MaxV Ultra.

"Best"
really means:

best among
the products we tested,

with our method,

at today's prices,

in this market.

Which is
a much uglier headline.

## the tests are asking different questions

Consumer Reports
makes the problem obvious.

Its current
"best robot vacuum" guide
is for vacuum-only robots.

Vacuum-and-mop combos
live somewhere else.

It also considers
privacy,
security,
predicted reliability,
and owner satisfaction.

The Hook Up
cares about
local control
and app behavior.

Expert Reviews
runs robots through
the same 58-square-meter home
with standardized
rice,
flour,
and pet hair.

WIRED
tests in a real house
with three adults,
a preschooler,
and a cat.

Which may be
the most efficient known method
for generating floor debris.

None of those methods
is obviously wrong.

But change the weights
and you change the winner.

## fake precision

Then there are
the giant suction numbers.

19,000 Pa.

36,000 Pa.

Numbers large enough
to make the box
feel professionally vacuumy.

But here's
a wonderfully rude example.

MOVA advertises
one model
around 19,000 Pa

and another
around 13,000.

In Vacuum Wars'
sealed suction test,

the 13,000-Pa model
measured higher.

1.08 kPa

versus

0.88.

Another current Roborock
advertised at 36,000 Pa

measured below
Vacuum Wars'
category-average suction

and still cleaned well.

So Pa
is not meaningless.

It's just not
a universal exchange rate
from bigger number
to cleaner floor.

## time is a test too

The other thing
most launch reviews
cannot measure

is Tuesday
six months from now.

One Ecovacs T90 owner
posted a very positive
first-week review.

Then updated it.

In week two,
navigation problems appeared,
areas went uncleaned,
and they returned it.

That one owner
does not prove
the T90 is unreliable.

It proves
a one-week review
cannot answer
a one-year question.

The Saros 10R
has the opposite advantage.

It's old enough
to have
three-month,
one-year,
positive,
negative,
and extremely annoyed
owner reports.

Some love it.

Others report
rug-fringe problems,
small-object failures,
dock issues,
or disappointing vacuuming.

That's not noise.

That's where
the hidden variables live.

## your house is part of the test

Take obstacle avoidance.

A repeatable lab course
with cables,
shoes,
toys,
and fake pet waste

is genuinely useful.

But a home
with little kids,
rug fringe,
reflective floors,
cords,
and toys
that migrate
while the robot is running

is a different experiment.

One long-term Saros owner
basically restricted it
to the kitchen.

Another runs theirs daily
and loves the avoidance.

Both can be true.

"Obstacle avoidance"
is not one number.

The house matters.

## so what should you actually buy

I am not going
to manufacture
a ninth overall winner.

But "it depends"
is not a buying guide either.

So start
with your failure mode.

If you want
a mature premium generalist,

the Saros 10R
is still
a useful benchmark.

RTINGS has it
at number one overall
and for hard floors,

and it has enough
owner history
to expose both
the good
and the annoying.

If carpet
is the center
of your house,

start with
Dreame X50,
Shark PowerDetect,
and Saros 20.

Different serious reviewers
pull those forward
specifically for carpet.

If pet hair
is the problem,

look at
Saros Z70,
Saros 20,
Dreame L40,
and the current
MOVA and Ecovacs leaders.

But separate
hair pickup
from
pet-waste avoidance.

Those are not
the same test.

If mopping
is why you're spending
flagship money,

look at
the newer roller systems

and pay attention
to the dirty-water path,

dock hygiene,

and maintenance,

not just
the beautiful stain test
on day one.

And if value matters,

ignore launch MSRP.

Use today's price.

A robot discounted
from $1,300
to $700

became
a different buying decision

without changing
a single screw.

## close

So why did
eight serious reviewers
pick eight different winners?

Different candidate pools.

Different tests.

Different weights.

Different prices.

Different dates.

Different homes.

The disagreement
is not the failure
of the review ecosystem.

It's the part
the buyer actually needs.

Start with
what would make
a robot fail
in your house.

Then listen hardest
to the reviewer
who actually tests that.

I put
the serious sources
in the description.

Not just the products.

The reviewers too.

If you want
another category pirated,

tell me which one
has turned buying
a normal object
into graduate school.

Headphones.

TVs.

Air purifiers.

Mattresses.

I'll go read it.

You can keep
your tabs closed.
