# Comparing film structures

## Recommendation

Develop **B — Map → scene → decision**, borrowing A's stable product identity and C's explicit buyer-specific branches. The house gets full-frame cinematic moments, but each is motivated by a particular piece of evidence and returns to a visibly changed comparison. This is a directing hypothesis, not an audience-tested winner.

The comparison uses the same Curv 2 Flow example in all three routes: The Hook Up's April seven-model award, double weighting of two cleaning categories, object avoidance score of 2/6, and the question of whether the viewer routinely leaves cables/toys out. No other model is declared a winner by surviving this illustrative beat.

## Ideas considered before the three mockups

| Structure | Strength | Weakness / decision |
|---|---|---|
| A. Otter beside a persistent evidence board | Highest continuity; no ambiguity about which product is discussed | Risks a charming lecturer beside slides. Keep as a clarity reference, not automatic choice. |
| B. Map → evidence → house consequence → map | Gives evidence and cinematic footage room; visible decision after each excursion | Requires disciplined return shots and persistent model identity. Preferred route. |
| C. Two versions of the same home | Makes buyer-dependent outcomes explicit; an action can change which tradeoff matters | Can branch too much and lose the original matrix. Useful within B, or for a very focused episode. |
| A literal elimination bracket | Very obvious progress | Implies commensurable head-to-head tests and a universal winner. Reject as the main grammar. |
| One room per product | Beautiful spatial memory aid | Arbitrary placement can imply each robot is for one room; a fake parade of demos obscures the review synthesis. Reject. |
| A wall of contradictory headlines / detective story | Immediate intrigue and evidence presence | Easily becomes accusation or conspiracy theater; too much reading. Keep one restrained opening complication, no detective identity. |
| A domestic deadline or big spill | Obvious character stakes | Advertises the category instead of differentiating models; makes tested-product claims easy to imply falsely. Reject. |
| A comic sequence of repeatedly changing the chosen product | Character has actions, setbacks and a resolution | Can look indecisive or contrived if facts are bent to produce surprises. Use one supported reversal, not a comedy treadmill. |

## A — The evidence room

**Hook:** the exact matrix from the thumbnail expands beside the otter, who is already engaged with it. No separate host introduction.

**Information flow:** the board remains on screen; highlight a model, enlarge the relevant review result, add the household condition, move its card. An eight-product strip keeps stable names/order.

**Character flow:** the otter sorts recommendation cards, follows a result with its gaze, and physically makes room for a revised choice. Its reactions punctuate the reasoning. Most shots are presenter-led.

**Then what:** viewers know the next chapter repeats this recognizable decision operation. Early source/method detail earns the right to simplify later.

**Risk:** too much split-screen makes the beautiful room a narrow decorative panel, and text competes with expressive acting. The film could be accurately organized but dull. Do not keep the same composition for minutes or rely on a moving mouth to count as cinema.

## B — Map → scene → decision

**Hook:** matrix first; a brief pullback or clean reveal places the otter in the approved room with the same comparison. Narration immediately says the work is comparing published reviews.

**Information flow:** establish the map, isolate one fact pair, cut to the household consequence, return to the same map with one labeled change. The names and the current buyer question stay recognizable across cuts; a full eight-name strip is used on board shots, while cinema carries only the current model and question.

**Character flow:** he reaches for an easy winner, encounters a meaningful caveat, looks at the corresponding condition in his house and changes the card's placement. The physical action embodies the decision. It is not a staged product test.

**Then what:** each return shows that the investigation has accomplished something. Sometimes the update is elimination for this buyer, sometimes a separate lane, sometimes an unresolved question. The endpoint is not predetermined by the edit.

**Risk:** cutaways can become beautiful filler, and disappearing labels force viewers to remember a model. Repair with one clear question before every excursion, a stable model name during it, and a visible board update afterward. Cut a cinematic shot if it adds no decision-relevant meaning.

## C — The same robot, two different homes

**Hook:** matrix becomes a fork: a clear-floor home and a clutter-heavy home. Same product, same evidence, different practical consequence.

**Information flow:** show the source result once, then apply the household condition. The clear-floor lane keeps the candidate for further comparison; the clutter lane sets it aside provisionally. This demonstrates why different recommendations can be rational without averaging scores.

**Character flow:** the otter tries tidying the cable, considers the recurring chore, and decides how much preparation he is willing to accept. Its actions reveal preference rather than pretending to measure a machine.

**Then what:** test another genuinely important buyer condition. Return to the matrix so the branches remain connected to published evidence.

**Risk:** more than two simultaneous lanes becomes a flowchart, and a room preference can be mistaken for an experimentally proved outcome. Keep one binary choice at a time; never imply clearing this cable guarantees the model performs well in other tasks.

## Common 30–40-second evidence beat

This is a proposed segment, not final recorded timing:

1. **Map / about 6 seconds:** “This one wins The Hook Up's comparison. Let's look at what that award includes.” Model and publisher are explicit.
2. **Evidence / about 10 seconds:** “Carpet vacuuming and hard-floor mopping count double. Object avoidance? Two out of six.” State the seven-model cohort and date visibly; do not animate a reliability percentage.
3. **Consequence / about 8 seconds:** “If you keep the floor clear, that tradeoff may work. If cables and toys stay out, it matters.” The otter looks at or handles a cable. No named robot runs over it.
4. **Decision / about 8 seconds:** “For that clutter-heavy home, I'd set it aside. It can stay on the clear-floor list.” Board update is labeled as our conditional synthesis, not the source's universal verdict.

The full episode needs a separate tested opening and a researched candidate ledger. This segment demonstrates one logic operation; it does not establish the complete final shortlist.

## Self-review and corrections before delivery

- A permanent eight-name strip over every beautiful shot was too busy. Keep the full strip on comparison frames; use the current model and question on cinematic frames.
- Showing a generic robot hitting a cable would imply a performance test. Removed the robot from the new household reference; the otter reacts to the condition instead.
- Treating 2/6 as a percentage would be false precision. Keep the source's category score and cohort context.
- A crossed-out product with no buyer label looks universally disqualified. Use a named set-aside lane and a persistent reason; the other lane remains available.
- A full matrix of “winners” is not supported by the existing coverage audit. The mockups label their source map as coverage; the final winner/recommendation layer requires its own ledger.
- The house shot alone does not establish credibility. Put a specific attributed result and a short method explanation before the excursion.
- The actual liked thumbnail was not recovered, so the mock opening represents its matrix concept, not a claimed pixel-perfect transition from the approved image.
