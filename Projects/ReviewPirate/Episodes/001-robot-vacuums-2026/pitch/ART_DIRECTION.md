# The house that changes the answer

Creative treatment, September 26, 2026. This is a proposed film language, not a model capability guarantee. The two generated concept sheets are still images, not a new video sample. They do not depict a real robot test or an exact commercial product.

## The experience

We enter a real-feeling home at the height of a robot vacuum. Oak flooring gives way to a rug. We travel into its fibers, then back out through the house. The kitchen, rug and cluttered hallway each ask a different question of the same purchase. We use the house as the organizing device: the viewer understands why a test matters because they can see the place where it matters.

The film moves between a believable domestic world and precise evidence. The room can become an architectural cutaway, or a camera can enter the carpet at an impossible scale. Those transitions earn their spectacle by explaining a buying problem. They do not depict a named machine passing an invented test.

The intention is a useful short documentary with a few extraordinary visual moments. It should sound like an informed friend who enjoys figuring things out. A buyer should be able to take the early answer and leave without being punished by a withheld verdict.

## Two treatments, with a recommendation

**A — A small, original otter guide in a cinematic house.** The otter is a fictional guide, not a claim of hands-on testing. Brown fur, cream chin, natural eyes, faded olive chore jacket; curious and dry, never frantic or squeaky. It is small enough to share the vacuum's point of view. It gives us an identifiable recurring presence and quiet physical humor. Its role is to ask the obvious practical question that a score obscures.

**B — The house itself is the character.** No on-screen host. A warm adult narrator accompanies low camera moves, extreme macro shots and an architectural cutaway. This avoids lip-sync and facial continuity work. It can still feel alive through camera motion, changes of scale, sound, and action. The risk is a beautiful but impersonal appliance advertisement; the script and editorial evidence must give it a point of view.

**Recommendation:** test A against B on the same 12–15-second excerpt. A is the more distinctive brand hypothesis; B is the stronger production fallback. If the otter seems childish, distracting, unconvincing in motion, or too costly to maintain, remove it. Do not downgrade its animation to keep the mascot. Nothing here locks a brand name or character choice.

This recommendation is a creative judgment. We have not measured audience preference for otters or validated that a mascot improves retention. A species popularity survey would not settle whether this particular performance works.

## Visual bible

**World.** An attractive but lived-in home. Oak floors, one cream medium-texture rug, muted teal door frames, linen sofa, real wear. No sterile showroom, luminous data lab, neon cyberpunk city, or stock luxury mansion. Household mess is ordinary, not disgusting. No invented results on the floor.

**Light.** Side daylight through a large window; warm practical lamp in the background. Maintain one motivated light direction within a room. Keep highlights from clipping on black product shells; retain texture in fur. Night sequences are not needed merely for variety.

**Camera.** Low 24–35mm views establish the floor as a landscape. 50–75mm equivalent medium portraits give the host space to perform without distortion. A macro sequence makes an embedded strand visible. Overhead cutaway shots explain spatial differences. These are compositional instructions, not a promise that a generator simulates a literal lens accurately.

**Movement.** One intelligible action per generated shot: a slow dolly across a floor boundary; a glance from a tuft to the lens; a gentle pullback to reveal the house. Cut on motivated movement or a sentence turn. Avoid instructing a model to walk, talk, pick up debris, transform a room and demonstrate a machine in one generation.

**Character.** Keep jacket pockets, cream chin, ear shape, tail, eye size and body scale stable. Do not put safety goggles, a lab coat, pirate clothes or a clipboard on it. Use a neutral expression sheet plus each necessary pose, not a collage of contradictory actions as the generation input. The concept sheet is for the owner; individual approved shot frames are what should feed a video model.

**Text.** Product names and all numbers are added in editing, not generated into the scene. One principal comparison at a time. Source credit appears near the relevant assertion; the full citation and method live in the description/evidence ledger. No wall of logos, drifting pseudo-text or repeated narrator pronunciation of long URLs.

**Color.** Warm wood, cream fibers, deep teal accents, graphite unbranded prop. The palette comes from the location. Avoid carrying the rejected Field Notes branding forward.

**Sound.** Conversational adult voice, unhurried but economical. No cartoon accent. Sparse tactile sound: jacket rustle, light paw contact, room tone, a cutaway transition. A quiet musical bed can establish momentum, then leave room for a surprising result. Never use sound to imply a named vacuum has a measured noise level. Source names require a verified pronunciation record before recording; on-screen credit can often replace spoken naming.

## Humor that serves the scene

Use a small physical reaction, not a mandatory joke per beat. Example: the host notices a loose hair on its own sleeve immediately after discussing pet hair, quietly brushes it away, then returns to the evidence. Do not have it imply an experiment was conducted. Omit the gag if it costs more attention than it earns.

An optional dry line for the maintenance section is: “The dock does a lot. It still doesn't empty its own dirty-water tank.” Use only for a specific dock whose manual confirms that fact, and omit for plumbed installations. No puns based on the species are needed. No “rug is mostly dog.”

## Critique of the concept sheets

The otter frame is substantially more expressive than the rejected renderer, but still has risks: the face may read young, the “thoughtful paw” can become a stock pose, and the detailed fur may drift across shots. The overhead room is an art-direction view, not a locked geometry reference. Its room details do not perfectly match the close-ups. The host-free macro frame is visually clear but could imply a particular measured carpet/hair setup if presented carelessly. Label the material illustration and cut to actual method details when making empirical claims.

These problems are actionable before video generation: lock the set from one approved master; create one adult neutral character reference; use separate shot frames; composite actual evidence independently. Do not pretend a still proves the performance will succeed.
