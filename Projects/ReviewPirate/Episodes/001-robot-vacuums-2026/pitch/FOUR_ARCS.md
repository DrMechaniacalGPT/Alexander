# Four stories before one film

This is the next development pass after the owner approved the otter and house. Establish the information and dramatic progression independently; choose camera/layout only after they support those stories. The exact liked matrix thumbnail still needs recovery. No animation, voice performance or finished candidate ranking is implied.

## 1. The factual story: what do we actually know?

**Starting question:** What can a buyer learn from comparing published reviews that one review alone does not provide?

**Beginning:** Establish the bounded landscape: eight core publishers × eight inherited models, with exact-model reviews, guide entries, related variants and unknowns distinguished. That is a coverage map, not a complete market census or an eight-winner table. Q7 M5 and Narwal Flow 2 are relevant additional candidates, so the episode's final universe must be reconciled before counting eliminations.

**Development:** Extract recommendations and the evidence supporting them. Comparable questions come first: overall convenience, carpet hair, hard-floor mopping, budget and upkeep are not interchangeable. Match product variants and preserve independent test families. Only show the methodological fields necessary to understand the current decision.

**Three factual units currently strong enough to develop:**

- **A winner with a relevant weakness.** In The Hook Up's April seven-model comparison, Curv 2 Flow wins overall; carpet vacuuming and hard-floor mopping count double; object avoidance scores 2/6. This explains how that award can coexist with an important buyer veto. It does not prove the scoring is dishonest or the robot fails two-thirds of the time.
- **A conflict we cannot fully solve.** Vacuum Wars reports Saros 10R carpet-hair pickup of 92%; RTINGS describes disappointing carpet-hair pickup. Missing historical protocol details prevent a matched comparison or causal explanation. Both sources can inform a cautious carpet-hair buyer without producing a meaningful average.
- **Convenience is a tradeoff.** Exact-model maintenance instructions establish that tank, filter and sensor tasks remain. This improves the buying question but is not evidence for a comparative reliability winner. The Q7 M5's simpler dock/bin arrangement is a separate, sourced workload tradeoff.

**Ending:** A conditional recommendation or small set of paths, with the strongest counterargument and direct original-source destination. The factual payoff is a better-grounded decision, not merely a compressed list of other people's scores.

**What must still be obtained:** a dated recommendation ledger for the chosen products/publishers; a defensible candidate inclusion rule; like-for-like historical/current price evidence where price changes drive a scene; accurate product assets. Do not script an exact final elimination count first and search for reasons later.

## 2. The decision story: how does the viewer make progress?

**Starting state:** too many plausible recommendations, uncertain relevance to this home.

**Step one — establish the options.** Show the matrix, then a manageable candidate board. Each product gets a stable short name, image when accurately sourced, and consistent place. Viewers are never expected to recognize similar black discs in room footage.

**Step two — group the questions.** Separate budget/basic vacuuming from premium automation and distinguish homes with different floors/obstacles. This reduces confusion without falsely declaring products losers. The current buyer question is always explicit.

**Step three — apply a real veto.** For a buyer who often leaves cables and toys out, a weak avoidance result can move a candidate to a set-aside lane. For a clear-floor household, that same candidate can stay in consideration. Keep the reason visible. Remaining products are unresolved, not automatically endorsed.

**Step four — compare survivors on benefits, price and work.** Check whether extra automation addresses a task this buyer dislikes and whether the current price changes the choice. Do not manufacture a common score from incompatible tests.

**Ending:** one or two understandable choices for a defined situation, plus a visible “we cannot confidently settle this” path when warranted. A viewer can name both the choice and why another appealing option did not fit.

**Progress grammar:** “Here are the options → here is today's question → here is evidence that matters → here is what changed.” Narrow products and claims for a buyer. Do not simultaneously run a tournament of reviewers; that creates a second landscape and encourages unwarranted attacks on sources.

## 3. The investigative story: why did the recommendations differ?

**Factual hook:** An overall award is not a promise of strength on every task. The Curv case has a real, documented explanation in category weighting; the Saros hair conflict does not yet have a verified explanation. That asymmetry makes the investigation more credible.

**Initial hypothesis:** perhaps the reviewers are answering different questions or assigning different importance to the same tasks.

**Test it:** show what the comparison actually counted. The viewer sees how an award and a weak category coexist. Then ask whether the answer transfers to the viewer's home.

**Complication:** a second case concerns the same broad task and model, yet assessments differ. We inspect methodology, versions and dates. We do not force the first explanation onto the second case.

**Other hypotheses worth checking:** different tested variants, publication versus test dates, changing price, firmware evidence, household conditions, independent testing versus repeated claims. Incentive disclosures belong in this audit, but an affiliate link is not proof of a purchased verdict. Old is not automatically obsolete; new is not automatically a retest.

**Ending:** classify the disagreement as an explained tradeoff, a context change or an unresolved conflict. Each classification leads to a different buying action. The intrigue resolves into useful judgment, not suspicion for its own sake.

**Do not promise:** eight distinct simultaneous winners; that every difference has a clean explanation; that we uncovered corruption; or that our synthesis eliminates the need for judgment.

## 4. The character story: what does the otter want, do and learn?

**Role:** a capable, mildly overconfident buyer-editor. He has already decided a robot vacuum might help. We are not selling him on the product category. He wants to make a choice he can stop thinking about and get back to his day.

**Beginning — the easy answer.** He finds a recommendation, places its product card on a small “my pick” stand and reaches to close the laptop. A second incompatible-looking recommendation catches his eye. He pauses. This beat depends on verified recommendation cards in the final film, not invented endorsements.

**Middle — the bad shortcut.** He tries to make one tidy pile of winners, then realizes the cards answer different questions. His actions change from collecting badges to sorting evidence. A tiny moment of recognition or an exasperated glance is enough; no compulsory joke.

**Middle — the personal consequence.** The house briefly reveals what a category means: he looks from an obstacle score to a cable near his chair. He picks up the cable, considers it, and recognizes that routine floor preparation is a choice of work. No branded robot is made to fail an invented test. The story is a decision becoming personal.

**Setback — still not a universal winner.** Another claim cannot be reconciled confidently. He leaves a visible question mark instead of forcing it into a winner pile. This is a character action expressing the scientific standard.

**Ending — a deliberate choice.** The large board has become a small, conditional shortlist. He puts away the irrelevant cards, leaves the decisive evidence beside the chosen path, closes the laptop and returns to his chair. Relief comes from understanding the tradeoff, not from a generated vacuum magically cleaning the house.

**Emotional arc:** easy confidence → overload → curiosity → better judgment → relief.

This gives the otter things to do while staying tied to the editorial story. Avoid an unrelated tea-party deadline, a catastrophic spill, a different robot for every room, or a fictional sequence of product failures. A quiet household detail can supply charm; it does not need to become a second plot.

## Connections that are earned

| Factual event | Change to the decision | Otter action | Visual opportunity |
|---|---|---|---|
| Several recommendation labels mean different things | Split incomparable choices into buyer lanes | Sort the “winner” cards into meaningful groups | Matrix cells reorganize into candidate cards; room remains visible |
| Winner has a weak obstacle category | Candidate may be set aside for a clutter-heavy buyer | Looks from evidence to the cable, then moves the card | Evidence close-up → beautiful floor-level shot → same board updated |
| Two hair assessments cannot be reconciled | Keep carpet-hair choice unresolved | Leaves the question open rather than stamping a winner | Two source cards, macro rug illustration, explicit uncertainty |
| Maintenance/current cost changes suitability | Compare the convenience actually purchased | Weighs a small task list beside the remaining choices | Exact sourced insert followed by a character reaction |

## What to test in the mockups

Hold the same Curv evidence and buyer assumption constant across designs. Change how map, evidence, house and character are edited together. Judge whether a first-time viewer can identify the model, the source, the comparison category and the consequence without remembering an earlier shot. Do not choose the prettiest frame if the argument disappears between cuts.
