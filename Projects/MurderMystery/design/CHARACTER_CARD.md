# Character materials: a playable person, not a database

Proposal; neither card length nor two-disclosure count has been validated. [Printable two-page sketch](character-card.html) is a typography/content prototype, not a complete game packet. The Alice material below is noncanonical, has no murderer or solution, and is unsuitable for sale or a standalone playtest.

## Structure

Use one quick-reference face plus a private guidance face. Keep private pages marked; “safe to share” means the player may disclose it, not that every other guest already knows it. Public introductions belong in a separate shared artifact. Longer background is optional only if essential instructions remain readable without it.

| Field | Job | Exclude |
|---|---|---|
| Identity / reason to attend | Let someone introduce themselves immediately | Long chronology required before first conversation |
| Outward manner / inner tension | Suggest a recognizable way to respond | Mandatory accent, symptom or performance-based clue |
| What matters tonight | Give competing commitments and a concrete immediate aim | A single objective score that resolves every dilemma |
| Relationships | Who matters, why, and who has a reason to approach you | Everyone connected to everyone by decorative history |
| Opening approaches | Two possible actions grounded in information already given | Commands that silently disclose new facts |
| Shareable background | Natural conversation material | Labeling unspoken facts as globally known |
| Private facts | Establish what is fixed in the fiction | Author/evaluator explanations revealing the solution |
| Disclosure guidance | Condition, content and authority of the instruction | An exact keyword unless the mechanic is intentionally a password |
| Freedom / boundaries | State what may be improvised and what remains fixed | A numbered moral-choice menu that implies only three valid moves |

Equal page counts or secrets do not imply equal roles. Review influence, inbound attention, reasons to act and useful choices after revelation for each role.

## Three kinds of disclosure instruction

- **Required fact:** a game rule requires a specific response when a defined situation occurs. Use sparingly where objective verification matters. A player violating it is a recorded deviation, not silently rewritten dialogue.
- **Permitted disclosure:** the player may now reveal something but is not compelled. The condition is permission, not a confession switch.
- **Discretionary response:** the player chooses whether and how to reveal; contextual guidance explains what they value and what is at stake.

Separate factual acknowledgment from cooperation. “Do not deny that the signed note is yours” need not mean “tell everyone why you wrote it.” Each secret should specify its own rule, avoiding blanket honesty instructions that accidentally eliminate all deception.

## Worked sketch: Alice

**Identity:** You are a meticulous organizer who wants others to consider you dependable.

**Optional portrayal:** When a conversation becomes vague, ask for one concrete detail. No accent or physical mannerism is required. Your precision sometimes masks a wish to be liked.

**What matters:** You owe Sofia a kindness. You also believe nobody should suffer for a false accusation. Tonight those commitments are pulling apart.

**Shareable background:** You and Sofia have worked together. She once helped you finish an important project when nobody else would. This is your account of the relationship, not evidence that Sofia is innocent.

**Private facts:** Sofia asked you to say you were together during a particular morning. You agreed. You were actually apart. An innocent acquaintance, Daniel, is now being questioned partly because people trust your account. Exact times, artifacts and connection to a murder remain unspecified in this sketch.

**Relationships and openings:**

- Sofia: find out what she thinks your protection is costing you, and what she is willing to explain.
- Daniel: ask what people are saying about him and what he needs. You may discover that the consequences are smaller, greater or different from what you expected.

**Disclosure A — the false account:** If someone provides a specific reason to doubt your story, address the discrepancy rather than merely repeating the same line. You may acknowledge the lie, seek a private conversation, ask what they intend to do with the information, or try another credible response. You are not required to explain Sofia's reason. You may disclose voluntarily earlier. Historical facts do not change because you improvise an explanation.

**Disclosure B — the promise:** If someone asks what you owe Sofia or what would make you stop protecting her, you may explain the promise and what it means to you. You may instead test their intentions. Decide how much to trust them. A general accusation does not mechanically compel this revelation.

**After disclosure:** You still have choices: repair harm to Daniel, ask Sofia to account for her request, seek corroboration, or negotiate how private information is shared. Nobody owes you forgiveness. Other roles must actually be able to respond; their eventual cards cannot be passive containers for your plot.

This example deliberately uses discretionary guidance, not two locked facts. A future fixture can include a clearly required factual response to compare the different mechanics. It must also specify reciprocal role instructions before it is runnable.

## How soft information enters play

Other characters may report kindness, selfishness, obligations or harm. Record who experienced it versus heard it secondhand, and whether the account can be contested. Do not label a card “Sofia is good” or compute a universal goodness score. Both people can deserve sympathy. A contextual detail is useful if it gives a player something to weigh or investigate, even when it does not change the murder's factual solution.

## Review questions

Can a reluctant performer introduce themselves and make a first move? Can an enthusiastic actor personalize the role without changing history? Can the player identify what is fixed, optional and private? Does each disclosure create further opportunities? Are there genuine consequences if the player chooses concealment? Could another role progress if Alice leaves? These require future reader checks and eventual play, not only a designer's confidence.
